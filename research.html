Research

My research centers on student engagement, and participation in classroom discourse, particularly in science learning. Across projects, I study both the structures of learning environments and students' experiences within them. The goal is to understand how teachers, peers, and classroom environments position students to contribute ideas, connect disciplinary content to their lives, and exercise agency over their own learning.

Classroom discourse, agency, and participation

If you've ever been in a classroom in any capacity, you understand how multifaceted classroom talk can be. Grounded in sociocultural perspectives on learning, particularly Vygotsky’s emphasis on language and social interaction as central to the development of understanding, my work examines how teachers and peers create, constrain, and negotiate opportunities for participation. I also draw on positioning theory to study how students are situated, and situate one another, as knowers, contributors, and decision makers through interaction. I am particularly interested in small-group science discourse, where authority, agency, collaboration, and uptake unfold moment by moment.

Key questions include:

  • How do students gain or lose epistemic authority during collaborative sensemaking?
  • What interactional patterns support productive and equitable peer discourse?
  • How do teacher moves shape the space students have to introduce, develop, and defend ideas?
  • How do classroom, group, and individual factors jointly shape participation?

Engagement, relevance, and students' funds of knowledge

I study engagement as a multidimensional and situated process. Engagement is not a stable characteristic of a student, and thus I examine how it shifts across lessons and learning environments. This work includes attention to behavioral, cognitive, affective, social, and agentic engagement.

A central strand of this work examines how culturally and locally meaningful experiences can become resources for science learning. Drawing on the hybrid discourse framework, I am interested in when students perceive that what they know from home, community, culture, hobbies, and everyday life is recognized as intellectually valuable in the classroom, and how those experiences relate to engagement, discourse, belonging, and achievement.

Computational approaches to classroom interaction

Classroom discourse produces rich, high-dimensional data that are difficult to analyze at scale without losing context. In addition to traditional quantitative and qualitative work, I investigate how computational methods can extend, rather than replace, theory-driven and qualitative analysis.

This work includes:

  • natural language processing of spoken classroom discourse
  • large language models for classroom observation and coding
  • machine learning for discourse classification
  • language coordination and linguistic feature analysis
  • mixed methods integration of computational and qualitative findings

I am especially interested in human-centered validation: determining when computational measures capture educationally meaningful phenomena, where they fail, and how researchers can combine automated methods with close analysis of interaction.

Research orientation

Across these strands, my work is guided by four commitments:

  1. Good educational research should be developed in partnership with teachers, students, and other stakeholders, and should answer questions that matter to them.
  2. Students are interpreters and creators of their learning environments, not just recipients of instruction.
  3. Discourse, engagement, and agency emerge through interactions among students, teachers, tasks, tools, and contexts.
  4. Differences in participation cannot be understood without examining how learning environments distribute opportunities to contribute and be taken seriously.
  5. Computational scale is most useful when paired with conceptual clarity, validity evidence, and contextual interpretation.
